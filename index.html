    Better days
    
    
    The beakless green feather was never meant to see better days.
    
    
    After the world underwent a nuclear holocaust most animals ceased to exist; the ones that didn't went through changes so drastic you couldn't even call them the same animal. The beakless green feather is an example of that. Its continuation of the green feather species is only by name and a couple of DNA mutations that tell us they are somewhat related, given that there is nothing green or feathered about the beakless. Its body works like a small atomic battery, turning the radiation from its surroundings into usable energy, and feathers would only hurt the efficiency of the process — so would eyes, ears, or any other sensory organs. Even today nobody knows how they communicated or mated; it feels as if they just appeared one day. Estimations are that their lifespan was about 300 years, but nobody knows for sure.
    
    
    The beakless's internal functions were a combination of several marvelous mechanics. One of its shticks was covering itself with water molecules placed so that the radiation splits them into hydrogen and peroxide, an order of higher potential energy, and then gathers them inwards — the new molecules small enough to seep through its flesh to the core, where they are mixed with bacteria that return them to their original form while the exothermic reaction feeds the body. Its heart stops itself when the hydrogen runs out, while keeping just enough charge to reactivate once enough has gathered again. This can happen anywhere between 4 minutes and 13 years apart, depending on how radioactive its surroundings are. Just like an electric automobile, it is speculated that it spends most of its lifespan dead.
    
    
    Eventually the radiation dropped low enough for things that resembled old life to begin forming. The last beakless green feather stood on the wall of Jerusalem.
    
    
    It couldn't see the green horizon stretching — a biological miracle, a win of life over death — so it started its engine one last time. The lack of radioactivity made it dizzy, and as it fell from the wall, for a second and a half the beakless performed something that had no survival benefit, something it wasn't programmed to do: reminding the world, and itself, that it is a bird. And birds fly.
